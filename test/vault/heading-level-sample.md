# h1. Harbor House Community Knowledge Hub

Harbor House is a fictional neighborhood learning space where residents can
teach practical skills, document local knowledge, and find trustworthy help.
This sample reads like a real project brief while making every heading level
visible in Outline View.

The literal `h1.` through `h6.` prefixes are intentional. They make it possible
to compare Markdown rank with rendered indentation without guessing.

## h2. Executive summary

The pilot will turn an underused library room into a weekly, resident-led
knowledge exchange. Instead of treating expertise as something delivered only
by institutions, the program recognizes that neighbors already know how to
repair bicycles, navigate public benefits, preserve family recipes, prepare for
storms, and help one another learn new technology.

The first twelve weeks should answer a focused question:

> Can a small, carefully facilitated program make useful neighborhood knowledge
> easier to discover, trust, and pass forward?

### h3. The opportunity

Residents currently find help through scattered social posts, private group
chats, paper flyers, and personal referrals. Those channels are valuable, but
they are difficult to search and often disappear when a volunteer moves away.

Harbor House can provide a stable place where people meet, practice, and leave
behind concise notes that the next participant can improve.

### h3. The proposed pilot

Run one facilitated session every Saturday morning. Each session combines a
short demonstration, hands-on practice, and a twenty-minute documentation
sprint. The resulting guide is published only after the teacher and one peer
reviewer agree that it is accurate and understandable.

#### h4. What success looks like after twelve weeks

- At least eight sessions have run without staff needing to supply the topic.
- Participants have published twelve concise, reviewed guides.
- Half of first-time attendees return for another session.
- Residents report that they can find a useful answer in under three minutes.
- No guide containing personal or sensitive information is published.

##### h5. Weekly signals worth watching

The team will record attendance, repeat participation, guide completion,
review turnaround, search attempts, and unresolved questions. These signals are
diagnostic rather than targets to optimize at any cost.

###### h6. A note about misleading early numbers

Opening-week attendance may be inflated by launch promotion. A crowded first
session is encouraging, but four ordinary Saturdays tell us more about whether
the program belongs in the neighborhood's routine.

## h2. People and responsibilities

The pilot needs clear ownership without turning volunteers into a bureaucracy.
Each role should be understandable in a single conversation and small enough
for someone to try before making a long commitment.

### h3. Resident teacher

The resident teacher brings a useful practice, a story about why it matters,
and a willingness to let participants try it. Teaching experience is welcome
but not required.

#### h4. Before the session

The teacher meets a facilitator for thirty minutes to define one achievable
outcome. “Understand home energy” is too broad; “identify the three largest
sources of standby power in your apartment” is testable.

#### h4. During the session

The teacher demonstrates once, narrates the difficult decisions, and then moves
into coaching. Participants should spend more time practicing than listening.

##### h5. Facilitation prompts

- What would you notice first if this were going wrong?
- Which step feels obvious to you but may be new to someone else?
- What can a renter safely do without tools or permission?
- When should a participant stop and call a professional?

###### h6. Safety boundary

Teachers must distinguish education from professional advice. Electrical,
medical, legal, and structural topics require an explicit stop condition and a
referral to qualified local help.

### h3. Session facilitator

The facilitator protects the learning environment. They welcome newcomers,
keep the session moving, invite quieter voices, and pause instructions that are
unclear or unsafe.

#### h4. The facilitator's most important job is translating expert shortcuts into steps a first-time learner can actually follow

### h3. Documentation steward

The steward turns session notes into a durable guide. They preserve the
teacher's voice while making prerequisites, warnings, and verification steps
easy to scan.

#### h4. Definition of ready for review

A guide is ready when it contains:

1. A one-sentence outcome.
2. Required materials and estimated time.
3. Numbered steps with observable results.
4. Warnings placed before the risky step.
5. A final check that tells the reader whether the result is correct.

##### h5. Editorial principle

Prefer a specific sentence that helps someone act over a polished sentence that
only sounds authoritative.

###### h6. Example

“The valve is closed when the handle is perpendicular to the pipe” is more
useful than “Ensure the valve is securely closed.”

# h1. Program experience

The experience should feel neighborly, calm, and purposeful. A participant
should be able to arrive alone, understand what is happening, and contribute
without already knowing the organizers.

## h2. Before someone arrives

Each event listing answers four questions immediately: What will I learn? What
will I do? What should I bring? Is this appropriate for me?

### h3. Invitation language

Use plain language and name the practical outcome. Avoid framing residents as
deficient or in need of fixing.

#### h4. Useful invitation

“Bring one utility bill and learn how to identify charges you can question.”

#### h4. Invitation to avoid

“Financial literacy remediation for underserved households.”

### h3. Accessibility preparation

Registration is optional, seating has clear paths, printed instructions use
large type, and facilitators describe visual actions aloud. Event listings name
known limitations before someone travels to the venue.

##### h5. Skipped-level test for an accessibility escalation path

This H5 intentionally follows an H3. Outline View should attach it to the
nearest preceding shallower heading without inventing empty H4 nodes.

###### h6. Same-day accommodation request

If the team cannot provide the requested accommodation safely that day, the
facilitator records the request, offers the material in another format, and
contacts the participant with a concrete next step.

## h2. During the session

The room opens fifteen minutes early. A visible agenda shows arrival, practice,
documentation, and closing times so participants can make informed choices
about their attention.

### h3. Opening ritual

The facilitator names the outcome, introduces the teacher, explains the safety
boundary, and asks each participant what would make the session useful.

### h3. Practice cycle

Participants work in pairs through a short cycle:

| Stage | Participant action | Facilitator observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Watch | Notice sequence and decision points | Which steps prompt questions? |
| Try | Perform the task with a partner | Where does confidence drop? |
| Explain | Teach one step back to the group | Is the language transferable? |
| Record | Improve the shared instructions | Did the guide capture the hard part? |

#### h4. Recovery when the demonstration fails

A failed demonstration is useful evidence. The teacher names what they
expected, what actually happened, and how they would diagnose the difference.
The documentation steward records the recovery path, not just the ideal one.

##### h5. Conditions for continuing

Continue when the failure is reversible, understood, and safe. Otherwise stop,
isolate the equipment or material, and reschedule the hands-on portion.

###### h6. Incident note

An incident note records facts, impact, response, and follow-up owner. It does
not speculate about blame.

## h2. After the session

Participants receive the draft guide within two days. The message includes a
single review question: “What would prevent you from using this on your own?”

### h3. Publishing workflow

The documentation steward incorporates corrections, the teacher confirms
technical accuracy, and a peer reviewer follows the guide without coaching.

#### h4. Versioning convention

Each guide shows its last review date, responsible group, and a short change
note. Superseded advice remains in history but is not presented as current.

# h1. Operations and governance

A trustworthy knowledge hub needs lightweight rules for deciding what belongs,
what needs expert review, and what should never be published.

## h2. Content boundaries

The hub publishes practical, locally relevant instructions that can be reviewed
by another person. It does not publish private case notes, credentials, door
codes, unverified accusations, or instructions intended to bypass safeguards.

### h3. Review levels

| Level | Example | Required review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine | Repairing a loose cabinet hinge | Teacher plus peer |
| Caution | Using a portable generator safely | Qualified reviewer |
| Referral | Interpreting an eviction notice | Link to professional help only |

#### h4. When classification is uncertain

Choose the more cautious review path. The cost of a slower publication is lower
than the cost of confidently publishing unsafe guidance.

### h3. Decision log

Material decisions include an owner, date, evidence considered, alternatives,
and a review trigger. The log exists to preserve reasoning, not to make every
small choice permanent.

#### h4. Example decision

The pilot will not require accounts for reading guides because the access
benefit outweighs the limited value of reader-level analytics.

##### h5. Review trigger

Revisit the decision if anonymous abuse, editing conflicts, or accessibility
needs cannot be handled through the publishing workflow.

###### h6. Evidence to collect

Record abuse reports, mistaken edits, support requests, and the number of people
who abandon a guide before reaching its first actionable step.

## h2. Twelve-week learning agenda

The pilot is successful if it produces a confident decision, including a
well-supported decision to stop or redesign.

### h3. Questions about participation

- Which topics draw first-time visitors?
- Which session formats bring people back?
- Who teaches, who attends, and who is still missing?
- Does the Saturday schedule exclude people we intend to serve?

### h3. Questions about usefulness

- Can someone use a guide without attending the session?
- Which warnings prevent mistakes?
- Where do readers ask for a person rather than another paragraph?
- How often does local context materially change generic advice?

#### h4. End-of-pilot decision

At week twelve, choose one of four paths: continue unchanged, continue with a
specific redesign, pause for a named dependency, or stop. The decision memo must
connect evidence to the choice and name what the team learned.
